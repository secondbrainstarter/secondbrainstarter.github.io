Distill, Don't File: How Notes Become Thinking

07. August 2026 · Method · English

You capture, you organize, you link — and the vault keeps growing. Growth is not the goal. A vault that only grows is a warehouse; a second brain has to produce thinking, and thinking does not happen in the filing step. The step that turns notes into thinking is distillation: rewriting the core of a note in your own words, smaller every time.

Filing is not understanding. You can file a quote you do not understand, and you can link two notes without having understood either one. Organization makes notes findable; it does not make them yours. A note stays a file until you have rewritten it — that is the difference between storing an idea and owning it.

Distillation is compressing toward understanding. Not summarizing for its own sake. You read the note, close it, and write the core in one or two sentences of your own — not the author's words, not your first draft, but the compressed version that fits your thinking. If you cannot write it, the note is not too long; it is too vague, and the fix is the note, not your patience.

Distillation is the step after retrieval. Closing the file tells you what you can actually recall; distillation is what you do with what you recalled. Recall without rewrite evaporates again, and rewrite without recall is copying. The two habits belong together: pull the idea out of your head first — the test of closing the file — then compress it.

Habit 1: one sentence, at the top. Every note that matters gets a visible one-sentence takeaway in its first line — plain text, no plugin. If you cannot write the takeaway, you have not understood the note; rework the note until you can. Thirty seconds of writing beats thirty minutes of reorganizing.

Habit 2: the review distills. In the weekly review, do not just check that the notes exist. Pick the three strongest of the week and rewrite each in two or three of your own sentences. Not more. Three per week is more than a hundred and fifty distillations per year — that is the difference between a vault that grows and a mind that grows with it.

Habit 3: the link text is distillation. When you link two notes, write the phrase that says why they belong together — the anchor text is a compressed thought. That is what always linking is really for: every link carries a small decision, and every decision is thinking.

What this is not. No color hierarchy, no progressive-summarization plugin, no markdown gymnastics. Highlighting is not distilling — highlighting is selection, and selection is the easy half. Plain text and one honest sentence beat any system of nested tags. If your vault needs a ritual to be understood, it is not a second brain; it is a cabinet with a ceremony.

Structure first, distillation second. Compression only works on a clean vault. A bloated collection with hundreds of orphan notes does not get better by being summarized harder — it gets better by being emptied, and the inbox rule is where emptying starts. Distillation is the layer on top of a small system, not a substitute for one.
